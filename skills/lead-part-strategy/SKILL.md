# Choosing the lead part

1. **Split the points first** from the scoring table: process points you control (rigour, reproducibility, presentation, test sets), points that move against a fixed baseline (results, gap closed), and points that pay for depth (lead novelty). Often more than half are controllable. If scoring is absolute, a rival with the same idea costs nothing directly.
2. **Scout each part from the problem statement, not from memory:** what it pays (measures, raters, ceilings), what the baseline is and how weak, allowed data, fallbacks for missing data, what counts as novelty. Grep for "points" and read only those ranges.
3. **Score on six facts (0 to 10):** results headroom, novelty headroom, feasibility on your hardware and time, independence from other teams and raters, open field in past research, synergy with the other parts. Show it as bars with the weakest link marked.
4. **Look for the weak baseline.** A baseline that does not fit your hardware, or an unfiltered baseline dataset, leaves cheap gains.
5. **When two parts are within noise, write a pilot gate** (4 to 6 hours each) with a numeric threshold, a default, and switch triggers (hardware offline, rivals crowding). Decide before the declaration deadline.
6. **Always cover the floor:** one guard and fallback wrapper over all parts, adaptations in at least two non-lead parts, one deep lead with controlled comparisons.
7. Re-read the problem statement for late-found rules (what "counts" as a learned contribution, what the reference for a rating is) before freezing the plan.
